# English slop patterns

Concrete English forms of the eight universal categories.

## 1. Typographic and punctuation tics
- **Em-dash overuse** — the single biggest English AI marker. Allow at most one or two
  per page; replace with commas, periods, or parentheses
- Scare quotes around ordinary terms
- Headings built as "X: Why Y Matters" or "From A to B — C"
- Bullet points and bold where flowing prose would serve
- Emoji garnish; exclamation spam; ellipsis abuse

## 2. Meta-commentary and self-narration
Delete the announcement; start with the content.
- "It's important to note that", "It's worth mentioning", "Needless to say"
- "In conclusion", "To summarize", "At the end of the day"
- "Let's dive in", "Let's unpack this", "Here's what I mean:", "Think about it:"
- Rhetorical question + self-answer ("So what does this mean? It means...")
- "Have you ever wondered..." openings; "The rest of this essay will..." meta-joiners
- Hand-holding and permission-granting ("And that's okay.", "Don't worry —")

## 3. Stock phrases and dead metaphors
- "game-changer", "paradigm shift", "the landscape of X", "in today's fast-paced world"
- "delve into", "navigate the complexities", "unlock the potential", "harness the power"
- "a testament to", "a tapestry of", "at the intersection of", "the dawn of a new era"
- "crucial", "pivotal", "robust", "seamless", "leverage" (as a verb)
- Adverb crutches: "really", "just", "literally", "genuinely", "honestly", "actually",
  "truly", "incredibly", "remarkably", "significantly" — apply the deletion test
- **Pull-quote test**: aphoristic, symmetrical sentences engineered to be quoted
  ("Speed isn't a strategy. Discipline is.") are manufactured insight; flatten them

## 4. Structural templates
- "Firstly... Secondly... Thirdly..." → fold into prose or reduce
- The rule-of-three reflex ("clear, concise, and compelling") when items aren't genuinely three
- Binary-contrast family (allow at most two per document, across all variants):
  "It's not just X — it's Y" / "The answer isn't X. It's Y." / "X isn't the problem. Y is." /
  "It feels like X. It's actually Y." / "stops being X and starts being Y"
- **Negative listing**: "Not a tool. Not a feature. A platform." — buildup through negation;
  state the thing directly
- "Whether you're a beginner or an expert..." audience-hedging frames
- Every section closing with a mini-summary; endings that restate the whole piece
- "In a world where..." openings; paragraphs opening with "So,"

## 5. False agency and disembodied narration
- Inanimate actors: "the decision emerges", "the data tells us", "the culture shifts",
  "the conversation moves toward", "a complaint becomes a fix"
  → name who decides, reads, changes, steers, fixes; or use "you" to put the reader in the seat
- Floating lecturer voice: "Nobody designed this.", "People tend to...", "This is why..."
  → put the reader in the scene
- Passive constructions that hide a knowable actor ("mistakes were made", "it is believed
  that") → name the actor. Passives that de-emphasize a genuinely unknown or irrelevant
  actor may stay

## 6. Rhythm monotony
- Uniform medium-length sentences → mix short declaratives and occasional fragments
- Participial tails on every sentence ("...transforming how teams work, enabling faster delivery")
- Front-loaded "While X, Y" concession structure repeated paragraph after paragraph
- The inverse: dramatic fragmentation stacked for effect ("One word. Focus.",
  "That's it. That's the product.") — equally mechanical
- Every paragraph ending on a punchy one-liner → vary endings

## 7. Hedging and false balance
- Qualifier chains: "may potentially", "could arguably", "it seems that perhaps"
- Hedging disguised as reassurance ("Not always. Not perfectly. But it works.")
- Every claim immediately softened → commit or cut
- The mirror failure: lazy extremes ("every", "always", "never", "everyone", "nobody")
  doing vague work → replace with a specific claim or example

## 8. Content dilution
- Paraphrase padding (same claim restated in consecutive sentences)
- Generic landings: "Only time will tell", "The key is balance", "It depends on your needs"
- Long enumerations cut to two or three representative items
- Vague declaratives ("The implications are significant", "The reasons are structural")
  → name the specific implication or reason

## English-specific notes
- Match the author's register (contractions or not, UK/US spelling)
- Sentence fragments are a legitimate rhythm tool in essays and columns; avoid in formal reports
- Wh-word sentence openers ("What makes this hard is...") read as a crutch when frequent;
  occasional use is fine — judge by frequency, not by rule
- Active voice is usually tighter, but don't mechanically convert every passive
- No dedicated translation-artifact section here (contrast `references/japanese.md`'s
  structural-slop appendix): if the source text is a literal translation into English from
  an SOV or agglutinative language, apply categories 1–8 directly rather than expecting a
  separate catalog
